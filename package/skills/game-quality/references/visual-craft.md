# 3D, shaders and VFX

Use the current project's renderer and conventions. Inspect its imports, material setup, lighting, postprocessing and asset loaders before adding effects. Follow the installed engine's documentation/skills for concrete APIs. WebGL GLSL, WebGPU/WGSL and a node-material system are not interchangeable; do not paste a shader for a different pipeline or add a second renderer to make an example compile.

## 3D: make the important shape read

Judge the object at its actual gameplay size and from the gameplay camera. Prioritize silhouette, proportions, distinguishable components and materials over tiny reference decorations that will disappear. Keep one clear hero and supporting geometry consistent with its scale and style.

For a reconstruction reference, describe a complete object with readable structure, a front three-quarter view, neutral diffuse light, sharp focus and clear separation of parts. Let the plugin request transparent RGBA. Avoid a collage, cropped wheels/limbs, motion blur, heavy shadows, scenic backgrounds and baked cinematic highlights. Do not alter a user-supplied reference or redesign its object without authorization. A single image leaves unseen surfaces uncertain; inspect those surfaces before assuming they are correct.

Review generated meshes for background planes, detached components, collapsed thin parts, wrong proportions, noisy paint, distorted wheels, lighting seams and implausible hidden geometry. Higher extraction density may preserve more inferred surface but will not invent missing evidence correctly. Hunyuan's High profile uses up to 200k triangles/2K atlas; a real test produced noisier paint than Balanced. Compare before replacing a usable asset. A larger atlas is not a new high-resolution diffusion model. The current adapter exports base color and surface normals; do not claim it produced separate normal, roughness, metallic maps, a rig or animation.

Integrate the exact returned GLB URL into the real gameplay entity. Fit bounds, ground the object, verify forward/up axes, and use a simple separate collision representation. Keep generated bytes intact; scene transforms, material overrides or a wrapper can adapt the asset without destroying the retained original. Do not subdivide a bad mesh merely to increase its polygon count. If a lower-cost derivative is needed, preserve the source and compare silhouette and materials at gameplay distance.

## Materials, light and composition

Give surfaces distinct physical roles: painted bodywork, rubber, glass, stone and metal should not all share one plastic appearance. Use the material channels actually present or deliberately authored; color should not be treated as roughness data. Reuse the project's color-space and tone-mapping conventions and inspect the result under the real lighting before adjusting exposure. Avoid processing the final color twice.

Compose a readable focal hierarchy. Use key/fill/environment light or the equivalent supported arrangement to separate the hero from its surroundings. Anchor objects with appropriate contact shadows or other grounding cues. Make brightness, contrast and fog help navigation and depth instead of hiding unfinished geometry. In a stylized game, simplify materials and shadows intentionally while keeping shape and value relationships consistent.

## Effects should explain the action

For each effect define its trigger, gameplay meaning, spatial attachment, onset, peak and decay. Build motion over time, not a permanent glow placed near the player. A boost can combine a short activation pulse, a speed-dependent trail and brief sparks at the exhaust; a hit can combine a contact flash, directional particles and a restrained impulse. Choose the layers that serve the chosen game's tone. Do not apply camera shake, chromatic aberration or bloom to every event.

Vary size, opacity, direction and lifetime with purpose. Keep trail emission tied to traveled distance or speed so it behaves sensibly at low frame rates and when stopped. Fade births/deaths without popping; pool frequent particles and cap concurrent effects. Reuse buffers/materials and avoid allocating a new mesh or material every frame. Clean up emitters, textures, listeners and passes when scenes restart. Clamp unusually large time steps after a suspended tab so effects do not explode on return.

Keep the player's silhouette, hazards, aiming and HUD readable during the strongest effect. Limit large translucent layers and full-screen passes; overdraw can cost more than the mesh count. Support reduced motion and avoid rapid full-screen flashes. Use measured performance to choose cheaper particle counts or effects, preserving the signature moment rather than disabling all polish.

## Shaders must be part of the scene

Choose a shader because it serves a material or action: coherent water motion, readable energy buildup, restrained heat distortion or a dissolving pickup. Prefer a small number of reusable, parameterized effects over unrelated demos. Use the project's time and coordinate conventions so world-space patterns do not slide accidentally and object-space patterns do not stretch unpredictably with scale. Make important speed, scale, intensity and color parameters easy to tune in code.

Use a suitable transparency/depth strategy and test effect intersections, sorting and camera movement. Include the needed project lighting, fog and tone-mapping behavior when a custom material replaces a built-in one. A compile-successful shader may still look wrong, disappear from one angle or wash out the scene. Test the strongest animation phase and combined effects in motion, check shader compile/runtime errors, and inspect the material at gameplay distance. Preserve a known-working scene if an optional effect is incompatible; report that limitation instead of silently dropping a requested signature effect.
